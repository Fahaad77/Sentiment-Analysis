Imagine you are a Hollywood producer who just released a major film on Friday night. By Saturday morning, over 50,000 reviews have been posted across IMDb, Twitter, and movie blogs. Reading through every single review manually would take weeks. How do you know - right now - if the audience loved it or hated it? This is where Sentiment Analysis comes in. Behind every online review, tweet, or rating is a human emotion. Sentiment analysis uses Natural Language Processing, or NLP, to allow computers to read thousands of text reviews, decipher the underlying sentiment, and instantly give decision-makers a clear picture of public emotion.

This presentation introduces sentiment analysis for a linguistics MA audience. The focus is not only on computer science, but on the linguistic question underneath it: how can a machine infer stance, evaluation, and emotion from text?

Definition and introduction

What Is Sentiment Analysis?

Sentiment analysis is an NLP task that identifies the evaluative attitude expressed in text.

Working definition: A computational method for classifying language according to polarity, such as positive, negative, or neutral, and sometimes finer emotions such as joy, anger, sadness, or trust.
  • It treats reviews, tweets, comments, and survey answers as language data.
  • It asks what stance the writer takes toward a topic, product, event, person, or idea.
  • It combines linguistic interpretation with statistical pattern recognition.
Start with the simplest definition: sentiment analysis is about recognizing attitude in language. For linguistics students, this connects to appraisal, stance, polarity, affect, and evaluation. The computational side adds a practical question: how can these meanings be represented in a way that a machine can process?

Significance

Why It Matters

People now express opinions at a scale no human team can read closely in real time.

ScaleThousands or millions of short texts
SpeedFast public reaction after events
Decision valueEvidence for action
  • Business: customer satisfaction, product feedback, brand reputation.
  • Media: audience reaction to films, campaigns, and public events.
  • Politics: public opinion, policy reception, and debate monitoring.
  • Research: corpus-scale patterns of stance and evaluative language.
The significance is not that sentiment analysis replaces human interpretation. Instead, it helps researchers and decision-makers locate patterns at scale. A linguist may still analyze nuance deeply, but sentiment analysis can point to where the major public reactions are forming.

Section 1

From Language to Data

The central problem: human meaning is continuous and contextual, while machine learning needs structured numerical input.

Now we move from the social motivation to the technical challenge. The key idea is translation: not translation between languages, but translation from human language into numerical features.

The linguistic challenge

Humans Read Context. Machines Need Representation.

A human reader notices sarcasm, tone, intensifiers, negation, and context. A basic algorithm sees strings until we convert them.

This slide gives students permission not to know the computer science yet. The difference is simple: humans naturally process meaning, while machines need a representation. Sentiment analysis begins by deciding what aspects of text are useful as evidence.

Research corpus

The IMDb Movie Review Benchmark

A classic teaching example uses 50,000 real movie reviews labeled as positive or negative.

  • 25,000 training reviews: examples the model learns from.
  • 25,000 test reviews: unseen examples used to check performance.
  • Binary labels: positive or negative sentiment.
  • Why it helps: movie reviews contain rich evaluative language, not only ratings.
The IMDb dataset keeps the Hollywood scenario concrete. It is useful because it gives us many authentic reviews and a clear label for each review. In machine learning, the training set is like guided practice; the test set is the exam.

Section 2

Three Techniques, One Flow

BoW counts words. TF-IDF fixes noisy counts. LSA moves from separate words toward hidden topics and meaning.

The next three techniques form a sequence. BoW counts. TF-IDF improves the counts by weighting distinctive words. LSA then asks whether different words may belong to the same hidden topic.

Technique 1

Bag-of-Words

Documents are broken into tokens; the tokens drop into one vocabulary bag, where order becomes less important than counts.

Review A

The sun is shining

Review B

The weather is sweet

Review C

The story is moving

the sun is sweet moving story
the is sun sweet story moving
the: 3 is: 3 sweet: 1 moving: 1
The first technique is Bag-of-Words. It is the simplest way to make text numerical. It treats a text like a bag: we put the words inside, ignore grammar and order, and count how many times each word appears. If a word appears five times, its value is five. The limitation is that very common words can receive high numbers even when they carry little meaning.

Feature vectors

The Sparse Matrix

BoW produces a large table of counts: documents as rows, vocabulary items as columns.

  • Rows usually represent documents or reviews.
  • Columns represent vocabulary items.
  • Most cells are zero because each review uses only a small part of the vocabulary.
  • Problem: common words can dominate the numbers even when they are not meaningful.
Bag-of-Words gives us a matrix. This is useful, but it creates a problem: words that occur everywhere can look important just because they are frequent. This prepares the transition to TF-IDF.

Technique 2

TF-IDF

TF-IDF solves BoW's biggest problem: common words are penalized, distinctive words are rewarded.

TF-IDF keeps the idea of word frequency, but it adds a second question: is this word rare across the wider collection? Common words such as "in", "from", or "movie" receive low weight because they appear everywhere. Distinctive words receive higher weight because they tell us more about the document.

LSA

Latent Semantic Analysis

LSA solves a shared limitation of BoW and TF-IDF: both can treat related words as separate signals.

Core idea Use SVD to reduce the TF-IDF matrix into fewer hidden dimensions called latent topics.

LSA comes after BoW and TF-IDF because it addresses meaning more directly. BoW and TF-IDF may not understand that "doctor", "hospital", and "medicine" are related. LSA takes the numerical matrix and uses a method called SVD to reduce thousands of word dimensions into a smaller set of hidden topics.

For linguistics students

What Sentiment Analysis Can and Cannot Hear

It hears lexical cues

Words such as excellent, awful, inspiring, boring, and disappointing can be strong polarity signals.

It struggles with pragmatics

Sarcasm, irony, indirect criticism, and cultural references often require context beyond word counts.

It depends on domain

"Unpredictable" may be positive for a thriller but negative for a delivery service.

Key point: A sentiment model is not a neutral reader. It reflects the data, labels, language variety, and assumptions used to train it.
This slide keeps the audience grounded in linguistic expertise. Sentiment analysis is powerful, but it does not automatically understand context in the human sense. This creates an important role for linguists in data design, annotation, error analysis, and interpretation.

Beyond classical baselines

Where the Field Goes Next

1

Lexicon methods

Use dictionaries of positive and negative words.

2

Classical ML

Use Bag-of-Words or TF-IDF with models such as logistic regression.

3

Embeddings

Represent words or sentences in dense numerical spaces.

4

Transformers

Use contextual language models that better handle meaning in context.

Ethical questions remain: privacy, bias, dialect fairness, annotation quality, and whether public emotion should be measured without consent.

Present the progression without overwhelming the audience. The field has moved from simple dictionaries to machine learning and contextual models. But better models do not remove ethical questions. A model can still be biased if its data or labels are biased.
